## The film in five lines

- **The want:** to beat his niece Pip at something, just once. On her seventh birthday she bets him she can hold her
  breath longer than he can; loser gets no cake. (In tickle-yourself she beat him at tickling.)
- **The obstacle:** his own body: an alarm at the base of his brain that he cannot switch off, a muscle that starts
  kicking, a body that would rather switch him off than let him win, and a seven-year-old who cheats.
- **The turn:** his shortcut (breathing fast first) is the one that drowns strong swimmers, and Pip blows the whistle
  on it. Doing it the slow way he nearly triples his time, then learns where the edge is: the record holders never do
  it alone, and if you hold it too long on land, your body takes the breath for you. The alarm he fought is the thing
  that has been breathing for him all his life.
- **The feeling at the end:** warmth and a laugh: he lets the birthday girl have the breath.
- **The final image:** dusk in the kitchen. Pip blows out her seven candles; the smoke curls up through the last of
  the sunlight; he is laughing; the clock flips from TOO LONG? to BREATHING; the theme's held note finally resolves.

- **Motif:** the seven birthday candles. In the hook they are the referee (whoever breathes makes them flicker); at
  45 seconds his explosion blows them out by accident (the worst thing you can do at a seven-year-old's party); during
  the breath count Pip relights them one by one; at the end she blows them out on purpose, with a wish: a held breath
  given away.
- **Wordless beats:** the candles going out and Pip's stare (2.2 s); Pip's nose (0.7 s, before "through her nose");
  the cold cloth SPLAT; the whale going down into the dark (2.2 s); the blackout (the screen goes black, 1.4 s); the
  breath count (13 s: Pip relights the candles one by one); the final image (2.6 s).
- **Colour script:** warm late-afternoon gold in the kitchen (cocky); the deep blue body with violet exhaust and red
  alarm light (the pressure rising); a black stage for the gauges (danger); cold teal for the cold cloth and the seal;
  sepia for 1960s London; the indoor pool's night blue; the abyss's black-blue with green specks (awe); black for the
  blackout; then dusk pink and candle gold in the kitchen (relief, warmth).
- **Score:** one theme, a rising arpeggio that ends on a held note it cannot resolve (a held breath): D F# A | E… On a
  music box in the hook, cut short by the first tick; over the seconds a ticking pulse with the held note as a high
  string that gets more dissonant as the exhaust piles up; jittery pizzicato for the fast-breathing trick, stopped by
  the whistle; glassy and slow under the cold water and the seal; an old upright piano for 1960s London; wide and slow
  for the records and the whale; silence for the blackout; warm for the count; only on the final image does the held
  note fall to D and the exhale line play out.

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges. The research
(15 Oct 2026) checked each claim against the papers or official pages below; where sources disagree, the script
says the safer thing.

| Claim (as said) | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Right now (about 18 s into a hold) your blood is still almost completely full of oxygen; when people let go, it is still more than 90 % full | established (saturation falls only a few per cent by the breaking point: about 3 % in Fowler's subjects, 2.7 % over 2-minute holds in air) | Parkes 2006, *Exp Physiol* 91:1 (https://doi.org/10.1113/expphysiol.2005.031625); Andersson & Schagatay 1998, *Undersea Hyperb Med* 25:21 (PMID 9566083); McCulloch et al. 2021, *Front Physiol* 12:731633 (https://doi.org/10.3389/fphys.2021.731633) |
| Every second your body makes exhaust (carbon dioxide); held in, it makes the blood a little acidic ("sour"), and sensors at the base of the brain detect it and drive the urge to breathe ("mostly the exhaust", not the missing oxygen) | established (CO2 is the main chemical drive; what finally forces the breaking point is not fully known, so the script says "mostly") | Guyenet & Bayliss 2015, *Neuron* 87:946 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.neuron.2015.08.001); Parkes 2006 |
| Most people let go between thirty seconds and a minute | established (untrained: rarely over 1 min in air, often 20–45 s; about 54 s after a full breath and 30 s after a normal one in McCulloch 2021) | Parkes 2006; McCulloch 2021; Parkes, *Scientific American* 2012 (https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/the-limits-of-breath-holding/) |
| The alarm goes off on the exhaust long before the oxygen gets low | established (at the breaking point CO2 has risen markedly while saturation has barely moved) | Lin et al. 1974, *J Appl Physiol* 37:291 (https://doi.org/10.1152/jappl.1974.37.3.291); Parkes 2006 |
| Fast breathing first adds no oxygen; it blows off CO2 so the urge comes late, and oxygen can fall low enough to black out without warning; in water this drowns strong swimmers | established (New York State 1988–2011: 16 cases, 4 deaths, all four 17–22, advanced or expert swimmers, all had hyperventilated; Craig 1976: 58 cases, all good swimmers) | Boyd et al. 2015, *MMWR* 64:518 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4584570/); Craig 1976, *Med Sci Sports* 8:171; American Red Cross Scientific Advisory Council (hyperventilation before underwater swimming should be prohibited) |
| Hand on your belly: it pushes out as you breathe in; that is the diaphragm, the main breathing muscle, a dome under the lungs | established | NHLBI, "How the lungs work"; Cleveland Clinic, diaphragmatic breathing guide |
| Cold water on the face plus a held breath slows the heart (the diving reflex), saving oxygen; a seal does the same on every dive | established (heart rate −7.8 % holding in air, −13.6 % with the face in 10 °C water; the film's monitor shows 78 → 70) | Andersson & Schagatay 1998; Malinowski et al. 2023, *Biology* 12:869 (https://doi.org/10.3390/biology12060869); Kooyman & Ponganis 1998, *Annu Rev Physiol* 60:19 |

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile, pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi;
pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with playwright-core and a Chrome or Chromium
(`CHROME_PATH`), and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. `.claude/skills/cp-long/bin/cloud-setup.sh` installs all of it on a
fresh Ubuntu machine. The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences
inside). Every sound is synthesized in `src/audio.py` (no recordings besides the narration). The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
