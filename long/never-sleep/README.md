# What Happens If You NEVER Sleep? (Day by Day) — long-form (N:NN)

**Final file:** `never-sleep.mp4` (not kept in git: it is on YouTube, and `src/build.sh` rebuilds it from this folder)
MP4 · H.264 High · 1920×1080 (16:9) · 30 fps · AAC 48 kHz stereo · N:NN · −14 LUFS integrated, ≤ −1 dBTP.
Captions for YouTube: [`never-sleep.srt`](never-sleep.srt). Thumbnail: [`cover.jpg`](cover.jpg) (1280×720).

A short animated film in the CurioPulse look. The hiker is at home in his blue hoodie on the teal couch (the living
room from tickle-yourself, without its calendar and rain), with a coffee pot, a red BOOK OF RECORDS and his ginger
cat, who sleeps through the whole film. The other worlds: inside his head (a heap of sleepy violet balls, the cell's
locks and a TIRED lamp, the brain as a city of lights), a night road, San Diego in 1964 on old sepia film (Randy, the
sleep scientist, a pinball machine, the chalkboard of sevens), a 1980s sleep lab with a rat, a giant fruit fly with a
see-through belly, brain tissue being rinsed, and the night sea floor with upside-down jellyfish. The narrator,
captions and bloom are the Shorts'. A clock in the top left counts the time awake (HOUR 16 · DAY 3) and flips with a
sound on every jump.

## The film in five lines

- **The want:** to be the champion of something. His cat sleeps most of the day; he will be the champion of NOT
  sleeping, and get his name in the record book (LONGEST TIME AWAKE: 11 DAYS).
- **The obstacle:** his own brain, which keeps the score of every waking hour and switches itself off, piece by
  piece, whether he likes it or not.
- **The turn:** the record book refuses him: nobody is allowed to try any more. What happened to the animals that
  went further, and the weirdest fact of all (jellyfish, with no brain, sleep), shows him that sleep is not the enemy.
- **The feeling at the end:** relief, and a warm laugh: the deep "finally" of lying down.
- **The final image:** dawn through the window. He sleeps on the couch with his cheek on the open record book; the cat
  is curled on his back, both breathing together; the clock flips from DAY 11 to ASLEEP; the lullaby finally gets
  its last note.

- **Motifs:** the record book (the thing he wants → the thing that refuses him → his pillow) and the cat (the joke in
  every home shot: asleep, always; at the end she was the champion all along, and he sleeps like her).
- **Wordless beats:** the first microsleep: the world skips, his mug jumps, the clock jumps, the sound drops (2.4 s);
  the coat on the door turns its head (2.3 s); the silence after "sixty-five" while Randy's chalk stops (2.2 s); he
  closes the book, looks at the cat, lies down (2.6 s); the final image (3 s, before the subscribe line).
- **Colour script:** a warm lamp gold on night one (cocky); colder blue as the hours pile up; a false sunrise at hour
  24; a sickly violet-green for days two to four (the floor breathes); sepia for 1964; clinical teal in the lab; deep
  ocean blue with glowing arms for the jellyfish (wonder); dawn gold for the end (relief).
- **Score:** one theme, a lullaby in F (3/4). In the hook it starts on a music box and is cut off by the coffee
  slurp before its last note; it comes back jittery over the caffeine groove, detuned and minor on days two to four,
  as a jaunty 1960s organ for Randy's pinball, glassy and slow for the jellyfish, and only in the final image does
  it get its last note (F). Silence for the stop after "sixty-five" and the gut reveal.

## The hook (reference/hooks.md, binding since 9 Oct 2026)

Three candidates, checked against the five rules and each formula's "fails when":
- **The Question** (kept): *"You chug a whole pot of coffee... so what happens if you just... never sleep?"* then
  *"Day by day... all the way to day eleven: the record nobody is allowed to try anymore."* The first sentence is
  the title's question in the words people type (rule 1); one idea, how far a body can go without sleep (rule 2); a
  specific number, day eleven (rule 3); the answer starts on screen by 0:20 (hour sixteen and the pile, rule 4); no
  greeting, no channel name (rule 5). Frame 1 is the thumbnail's picture: his face and the coffee pot, mid-chug.
- *Someone Else's Result*: "In 1964, a seventeen-year-old stayed awake eleven days straight..." Strong and sourced, but
  its first sentence is about Randy, not "you", and it spends the film's proof in the first second.
- *The Statistic*: "Seventeen hours awake hits you like a few drinks." Sourced (Dawson & Reid 1997), but it confirms
  the title only in the second sentence and makes two promises (driving and never sleeping).
The last films' formulas: tickle-yourself opened on a dare ("Go on. Try to tickle yourself"), so this is not a fourth
in a row of one formula.

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim (as said) | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| The longer you're awake, a chemical (adenosine) piles up in the brain and you feel sleepier; coffee (caffeine) blocks its receptors, so you can't feel the pile | established | Porkka-Heiskanen et al. 1997, *Science* 276:1265 (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.276.5316.1265); Fredholm et al. 1999, *Pharmacol Rev* 51:83 |
| Seventeen hours awake impaired people about as much as a 0.05 % blood alcohol level (the limit in many countries); ~24 h ≈ 0.10 % | established | Dawson & Reid 1997, *Nature* 388:235 (https://doi.org/10.1038/40775); Williamson & Feyer 2000, *Occup Environ Med* 57:649 |
| At sunrise you get a "second wind" from the body clock, not because the sleep pressure went away | established (two-process model) | Borbély 1982, *Hum Neurobiol* 1:195; Borbély et al. 2016, *J Sleep Res* 25:131 (https://doi.org/10.1111/jsr.12371) |
| After one sleepless night, the amygdala (the brain's alarm centre) reacted about 60 % more to upsetting pictures | established finding; "about 60 %" is the authors' own figure from the press release | Yoo et al. 2007, *Curr Biol* 17:R877 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2007.08.007); UC Berkeley release 22 Oct 2007 |
| After a sleepless night people want junk food more | established (small studies) | Greer, Goldstein & Walker 2013, *Nat Commun* 4:2259 (https://doi.org/10.1038/ncomms3259) |
| Patches of the brain fall asleep while you're awake; microsleeps of a few seconds that you often don't notice | established in rats and humans | Vyazovskiy et al. 2011, *Nature* 472:443 (https://doi.org/10.1038/nature10009); Nir et al. 2017, *Nat Med* 23:1474 (https://doi.org/10.1038/nm.4433) |
| Drowsiness at the wheel causes crashes | established | NHTSA, Drowsy Driving (https://www.nhtsa.gov/risky-driving/drowsy-driving) |
| After days without sleep people see things that aren't there | established (review of 21 studies) | Waters et al. 2018, *Front Psychiatry* 9:303 (https://doi.org/10.3389/fpsyt.2018.00303) |
| Randy Gardner, 17, San Diego, Dec 1963 – Jan 1964, a school science fair project, 264.4 h (11 days 24 min); William Dement of Stanford came to watch; moody, forgetful, hallucinating within days; they played pinball and basketball and Randy kept beating Dement; on day 11 he stopped counting down by sevens at 65 and said he'd forgotten what he was doing; he slept about 14 h and was medically fine | well documented (Ross was the Navy physician who reported the neurology) | Ross 1965, *Arch Neurol* 12:399 (https://doi.org/10.1001/archneur.1965.00460280069006); NPR, 28 Jan 2024 (https://www.npr.org/2024/01/28/1227217274/sleep-deprivation-record); https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randy_Gardner_sleep_deprivation_experiment |
| The record book stopped accepting attempts at staying awake (safety) | established (Guinness's own explanation) | Guinness World Records, "What's the limit to how long a human can stay awake? And why we don't monitor the record", Jan 2023 (https://www.guinnessworldrecords.com/news/2023/1/whats-the-limit-to-how-long-a-human-can-stay-awake-733188) |
| Rats kept awake for good died within weeks (11–32 days; usually two to three), and no single cause was found | established | Everson, Bergmann & Rechtschaffen 1989, *Sleep* 12:13 (https://doi.org/10.1093/sleep/12.1.13); Rechtschaffen & Bergmann 2002, *Behav Brain Res* |
| In 2020 a Harvard lab found sleep-deprived flies die with damage (reactive oxygen species) building up in the gut; antioxidants (fed, or made in the gut) let flies live a normal lifespan with little sleep | established in flies; mice showed the gut damage, not the deaths | Vaccaro et al. 2020, *Cell* 181:1307 (https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cell.2020.04.049); Harvard Gazette, June 2020 |
| One idea: during sleep fluid rinses waste out of the brain (mice); a 2024 mouse study found clearance was lower in sleep; still argued | debated (said as "one idea" and "still arguing") | Xie et al. 2013, *Science* 342:373 (https://doi.org/10.1126/science.1241224); Miao et al. 2024, *Nat Neurosci* 27:1046 (https://doi.org/10.1038/s41593-024-01638-y); Alzforum coverage of the dispute |

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile, pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi;
pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with playwright-core and a Chrome or Chromium
(`CHROME_PATH`), and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. `.claude/skills/cp-long/bin/cloud-setup.sh` installs all of it on a
fresh Ubuntu machine. The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences
inside). The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of
`src/script.txt` changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
