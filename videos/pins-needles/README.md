**Title:** Why Does Your Foot Fall ASLEEP? 🦶

**Description:**
You sit cross-legged for twenty minutes, stand up... and your leg is gone. Then comes the fizz: pins and needles.

It isn't really your circulation. Sitting like that squashes a nerve, and the tiny blood vessels that feed it. Starved,
the nerve stops sending signals, so your brain can't feel the foot or tell where it is. When you move, blood rushes
back and the nerve's fibres fire by themselves for a minute or two: scientists have recorded them firing in bursts of
up to 300 impulses a second, with nothing touching the skin. Your brain reads that static as pins and needles.
Subscribe for a new strange question every day.

It is harmless when it passes in a few minutes. Numbness or tingling that lasts, or keeps coming back for no reason,
is one for a doctor.

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A limb "falls asleep" because sustained pressure squashes a nerve and the small blood vessels that supply it, not because the limb's blood is cut off ("You didn't cut off your circulation. You squashed a NERVE... and the tiny vessels that feed it.") | established | Harvard Health, "When nerves get damaged" (2024): "The abnormal pressure pushes on nerves and compresses the blood vessels that supply them." Nemours KidsHealth, "Why Does My Foot Fall Asleep?": "Many people say this is because you've cut off the blood supply to your foot, but your nerves are more to blame." NINDS paresthesia page (as reproduced in MedLink Neurology's handout): it "happens when sustained pressure is placed on a nerve" |
| While it is squashed, the nerve conducts poorly: the limb goes numb, heavy and clumsy, because touch and position signals no longer arrive ("Starved, it goes quiet. Your brain can't feel the foot... or find it.") | established | R. H. Shmerling, Harvard Health (2020), "Hands or feet asleep? What to do": "It feels dead, heavy, and simply won't work." MSD Manual, Numbness (loss of sensation and of position sense as sensory symptoms) |
| When the pressure comes off and blood returns, the sensory fibres fire on their own; that is the pins and needles ("the nerve reboots... badly") | established | J. L. Ochoa and H. E. Torebjörk, "Paraesthesiae from ectopic impulse generation in human sensory nerves", Brain 103 (1980) 835–853. I. Mogyoros, H. Bostock and D. Burke, "Mechanisms of paresthesias arising from healthy axons", Muscle & Nerve 23 (2000) 310–320 (after the blood returns the fibre's membrane is briefly unstable and impulses start by themselves) |
| "Scientists have recorded its fibres firing by themselves, hundreds of times a second." | established | Ochoa and Torebjörk 1980: microelectrodes in the nerves of awake volunteers; spontaneous activity began about half a minute after the circulation returned and peaked in the second minute; single fibres fired in bursts of 200–300 impulses a second lasting 1–7 s |
| "Nothing is touching your skin." | established | Ochoa and Torebjörk 1980: local-anaesthetic blocks showed the impulses were generated along the nerve fibres that had been starved, "and not primarily from the receptors in the skin" |
| "It's static... and your brain guesses: needles?" | a way of putting it (stamped BEST GUESS on screen) | Ochoa and Torebjörk 1980 propose that the odd quality comes from "an abnormal spatio-temporal pattern of ectopic impulses from different types of sensory units, creating a chaotic percept" |
| "It's harmless. Wiggle your toes..." | established for the occasional, brief kind | Shmerling, Harvard Health (2020): rarely anything to worry about when it follows holding one position and passes within minutes; see a doctor for persistent numbness or tingling (said in the description) |

Simplifications:
- The x-ray is a schematic folded leg. In real cross-legged sitting the nerve most often squeezed is the common peroneal
  nerve, at the outside of the knee; the picture shows "a nerve in the fold" without naming it.
- "Reboots", "ERROR", "NO SIGNAL" and "static" are metaphors for a conduction block and for ectopic firing. The scope
  trace is drawn, not data; the counter stops at 300, the top of the range the 1980 paper reports.
- The first pins and needles can also start while the limb is still squashed (G. Weddell and D. C. Sinclair, "Pins and
  needles", J Neurol Neurosurg Psychiatry 10 (1947) 26–46). The Short tells the familiar order: numb, then the fizz.
- Not used: the medical name for a limb falling asleep, obdormition (cut for length).

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 10 minutes. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile,
pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with
playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are
vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). No recorded one-shots are used in this Short.
The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
`build.sh` also renders the cover (`SC.cover`, from a one-shot copy of the timeline) into `cover.jpg`.
