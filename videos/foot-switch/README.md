   the foot flips round to the 6's way. A test anyone can do in three seconds with the phone in the other hand, and it

3. **Why this topic tonight:** the ring-finger Short (16 Oct 11:30) has 16,682 views, about seven times the middle

## Narration (75 words, performed)

Directed in [`src/script.txt`](src/script.txt), one take per block. The joke timing sits in the gaps between blocks.

> Circle your right foot clockwise, and draw a six with your right hand.
> Yep, your hand just HIJACKED your foot.
> Your right hand and foot share a boss... the LEFT side of your brain.
> It wants them going the SAME way, so the six drags your foot along.
> Now try the six with your LEFT hand. Easier, right?
> Weirder? In one study, people fumbled it just IMAGINING it.
> Your foot's not broken. It's a copycat.

The hook was auditioned in six wordings (`voice.synth`): the two-sentence "...clockwise. Now draw a six..." ran 4.9–5.4 s
and pushed the answer past 5 s; one sentence with "and" (seed 5) ran 4.4 s, 4.1 s at tempo 1.06.

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Moving the hand and the foot of the same side together is easy in the same direction, and hard or impossible in opposite directions; an opposite pairing "tends to reverse spontaneously to the easy pattern" | established | Baldissera, Cavallari & Civaschi, *Neuroscience Letters* 34 (1982) 95–100; Baldissera & Esposti, *NeuroReport* 16 (2005); McIntyre-Robinson & Byblow, *J Neurophysiol* 110 (2013) 1039–1046 |
| "So the six drags your foot along": the 6's loop goes counter-clockwise, against the clockwise foot, and the foot gives way to the 6's direction | established as behaviour (the trick is the classic demonstration of the rule above); the circle-and-6 version itself has no paper of its own | the sources above; the trick is widely reported (e.g. greenwoodcalendar.com "Curiosity Corner: Clockwise, Counterclockwise") |
| Your right hand and right foot take their orders from the left side of the brain ("share a boss") | established (each side of the motor cortex runs the opposite side of the body) | any neuroscience textbook; NINDS "Brain basics" |
| Why the two orders interfere is not settled (signals that leak between the hand's and the foot's plans, the brain's postural adjustments, or how we perceive direction): the Short says only that the brain "wants them going the same way", i.e. the preference itself, not its cause | debated | Baldissera & Esposti 2005 (postural adjustments); Nakagawa et al., *Front Hum Neurosci* 2023 (signals to the resting hand rise and fall with the foot); Mechsner et al., *Nature* 414 (2001) (perception) |
| The same six with the LEFT hand (right foot still circling) is easier | established for the paired movement studied in the lab (wrist and ankle flexing): performance was lower for the same-side pair than for the opposite-side pair | Nakagawa, Kawashima, Mizuguchi & Kanosue, *Front Hum Neurosci* 10:499 (2016) |
| "TWO BOSSES": the left hand is easier because its orders come from the other side of the brain (the picture shows a second boss) | leading idea: the study's authors link the difference to how strongly the brain holds back the opposite-way pattern in the supplementary motor area, which they call speculation | Nakagawa et al. 2016 (above) |
| "In one study, people fumbled it just IMAGINING it": opposite-direction hand-foot movements took longer than same-direction ones even when both were only imagined | one study (hedged in the words: "in one study") | Nakagawa et al., *Front Hum Neurosci* 17:1133279 (2023): "The opposite directional movement took a significantly longer time than did the same directional movement, irrespective of the condition type" |
| The rule follows the direction in space, not which muscles work (it holds with the hand palm up or palm down) | established (not in the script; kept for the comments) | Baldissera et al. 1982 |

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 10 minutes. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile,
pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with
playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The cinematic lighting runs on the graphics
chip through Chrome's WebGL2; without one the render falls back to the classic look and says so (`look: ...`). The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are
vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). Recorded one-shots come from the repo's shared CC0 folder
`assets/sfx/` (`git sparse-checkout add assets/sfx`; credits in `assets/sfx/CREDITS.md`).
The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
