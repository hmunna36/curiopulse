**Title:** Why Is One Nostril ALWAYS Blocked? 👃

**Description:**
You pinch one nostril shut... and the other one is blocked. Relax: that's not a cold, your nostrils work in shifts,
and that one is on its break. Subscribe for a new strange question every day.

How it works: the inside of your nose is lined with spongy tissue that can fill with blood. One side swells and
nearly shuts its passage while the other shrinks and opens wide, and every few hours they swap. It is called the nasal
cycle. It was first measured in 1895, and in a 2016 study that tracked 33 people's breathing for a day everyone had
it: a shift lasted about two hours on average, but anything from 15 minutes to 10 hours. You mostly notice it with a
cold, when everything in there is already swollen. Why the nose does it is not settled; one idea is that each side
gets to rest and stay moist. And because one nostril has fast air and the other slow, each smells the world a little
differently: in a 1999 study in Nature, the same mix of a minty smell and a fuel smell seemed mintier through the fast
nostril and more like fuel through the slow one. A nostril that is blocked all the time, or always on the same side,
is one for a doctor.

## Sound design

- **The nose is the instrument** (`sniff` in `src/sfxkit.py`, added for this Short): a sniff is a quick rush of air
  that cuts off; where nothing gets through it narrows to a thin whistle that climbs. Three of them, each harder, run
  under the hook: in the pauses whole, under the words only what is above 5.2 kHz.
- **Beds:** the bathroom (low air), in the morning and in the evening; inside his nose a muffled low pulse and a thin
  breath of air above the words.
- **Hits:** every sound that carries a joke sits in a pause of the narration: the finger and the first sniff before
  "You", a stuffed honk after "blocked.", a clean sniff after "Relax,", a shop bell after "shifts,", a snore after
  "break.", a balloon's squeak after "shut,", a breath out after "wide.", a ratchet (the clock, wound on) after
  "hours...", the shift bell after "swap.", a sniffle before "You mostly", wah-wah after "both.", the jar after
  "Weirder:", a zip after "fast,", a lazy slide after "slow,", a bright note and a sour one after "differently.", two
  taps after "Go on...", the shift bell again after "now." and a sparrow outside. What happens ON a word is on the bed
  bus and is either under 250 Hz (the sniff stopping, the signs landing, the pulse of the blood, the rubber
  stretching, the swap) or above 5 kHz (the air, the clock's ticks, the labels).
- **Score:** a light pizzicato groove for the morning that stops when the sniff does and comes back under "your
  nostrils work in shifts"; nothing under "on its break"; a curious minor loop inside, with a hole for the squeak; a
  quick bright one for the clock, cut off for the ratchet and the bell; a slow sorry one for the cold; a sneaky walk
  (bass and snaps) for the jar, with holes for the zip and the slide; the morning groove again under "check who's on
  shift now", and one soft chord before the loop.
- **Mix:** voice first (leveler, gentle compression), effects duck under speech, −14 LUFS, true peak ≤ −1 dBTP
  after AAC. The music bus sits 3 dB under the house level and ducks a little more (`levels={"music": -14}`,
  `duck={"music": 0.74}`): this narration's line endings are soft. `src/qa.py` on the delivered file: see "Ship
  review". `src/qc_gags.py` lists every joke sound's level in its own pause.

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "You pinch one nostril shut and the other one's blocked": at any moment more air goes through one nostril than the other; the side with less can feel blocked | established. In healthy people the low side is partly, not fully, shut: the picture shows a plug with a dark edge round it, the narration says "nearly shut" a few seconds later, and the description says that a nostril that is always blocked is for a doctor | Kahana-Zweig et al. 2016; Cleveland Clinic |
| "Your nostrils work in shifts, and that one's on its break": the two sides take turns being the open one (the nasal cycle), without your doing anything | established: first measured by Richard Kayser in 1895; in a study of 33 healthy people who wore a recorder for 24 hours every one of them cycled; the autonomic nervous system runs it. "Shifts" and "break" are the script's picture of it | Kahana-Zweig et al. 2016; Kayser 1895 |
| "Spongy tissue inside fills with blood and swells one side nearly shut, while the other opens wide": blood fills the erectile tissue of the shelves on the side wall (the turbinates, chiefly the lowest) and of the front of the dividing wall on one side, while the other side's empties | established. The section on screen is a drawing, not to scale: three shelves a side, the wall between; the faces, the hard hat and the signs are jokes | Kahana-Zweig et al. 2016; Cleveland Clinic |
| "Every few hours... they swap": on screen "~ 2 HOURS" and "IT VARIES A LOT" | established, with the spread said on screen: the average time one nostril led was 2.15 hours (± 1.84), from 15 minutes to 10.35 hours; shorter awake (about 2 hours) than asleep (about 4.5). Clinics say "every four to six hours". There is no fixed timetable, and what sets the rhythm is not fully known | Kahana-Zweig et al. 2016; Cleveland Clinic |
| "You mostly notice when a cold swells both": with a cold the lining of both sides is swollen, so the side whose turn it is to be narrow can close completely | the usual explanation, said with "mostly": people with a healthy nose rarely notice the cycle; a cold (or lying on one side, which fills the lower nostril) makes it obvious | Cleveland Clinic; Kahana-Zweig et al. 2016 (posture); Wikipedia, "Nasal cycle" (the cold) |
| "One fast, one slow, so each nostril smells the world a little differently": people smelled a mix of two odorants, one that the nose's lining soaks up quickly (L-carvone, spearmint) and one it soaks up slowly (octane, a fuel smell), through each nostril in turn. Through the nostril with more airflow the mix seemed to hold more carvone; through the one with less, more octane. On screen: FAST, SLOW, a mint leaf, a fuel can, ONE STUDY, 1999 | established as that study's finding; the differences are subtle, and "a little" and the on-screen label say so. The mint leaf and the can stand for the two smells of that experiment, not for what a blocked nostril does to every smell | Sobel et al. 1999; New Scientist 1999 |
| "Go on... check who's on shift now." | an invitation: pinching one nostril and then the other shows which side is leading | — |

Not in the Short: why the nose does it (not settled: resting and re-wetting one side at a time is one idea; a wider
range of smells is another), the lower nostril filling when you lie on your side, and the idea that the leading
nostril tracks which half of the brain is busier (not supported by later work).

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 6 minutes; `src/cover.sh` then renders `cover.jpg`. It needs
python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile, pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic
chain runs), node with playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The cinematic lighting
runs on the graphics chip through Chrome's WebGL2; without one the render falls back to the classic look and says so
(`look: ...`). The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside).
The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
