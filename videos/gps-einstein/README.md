# Your Phone's GPS Needs EINSTEIN 🛰️ — Short (28 s)

**Final file:** [`gps-einstein-short.mp4`](gps-einstein-short.mp4)
MP4 · H.264 High · 1080×1920 (9:16) · 30 fps · AAC 48 kHz stereo · 27.8 s · −14 LUFS integrated, ≤ −1 dBTP.
Captions for YouTube: [`gps-einstein.srt`](gps-einstein.srt). Cover: [`cover.jpg`](cover.jpg).

A CURIOUS Short (the Curious queue's first line, promised by honey-never-goes-off's "Next up"), on the tight arm (25–30 s, the rules
of 5 Nov 2026), framed as a comparison of time: up in space, time runs faster than in your pocket, by 38 millionths of a second a
day, and that is ten kilometres of error for your phone's map. The hiker checks his map at a mountain lake at dusk, floats up beside a
GPS satellite to compare watches, follows his drifting blue dot off the end of a jetty into the lake, and finally flips the 1977 test
satellite's FIX switch on himself.
The look is the cinematic one (`publish.json` "look": "cine"): the lake is lit by the afterglow of a sun just set on the right
(`LIGHTS.sunset`), orbit by the Sun high on the left with the Earth's blue glow from below (`GP_L_SPACE`). The cinema kit plans the
camera: 4 whips, 3 punch-ins, a tilt, 2 hitstops, moods day → night → cold → night → dusk → shade → warm → day.

## The plan (story.md §5)

1. **Length:** tight arm, 25–30 s, 55–64 words (`yt.mjs next-slot`: TIGHT, slot 16 Nov 2026 23:30 IST, no pill, topic kind CURIOUS).
   57 words, 27.8 s (the first draft, 61 words, ran 31.9 s: "Because", "For GPS" and "switched" came out, and the pauses were capped).
2. **Hook formula: The Impossible Claim told through the viewer's own phone** (after The Direct Address (m-mouth-open), The Question
   (tongue-clover), The Comparison (honey-never-goes-off)): "Your phone's GPS needs EINSTEIN." It IS the title, said to the viewer about
   the phone in their hand. Frame 0: a tight shot of him holding his phone up beside his face at a mountain lake, the map app on it (his
   head about 370 px tall); on "GPS" a dashed beam climbs from the phone to a satellite glinting in the sky; on "EINSTEIN" his hair poofs
   into a white cloud with a moustache (the gag), and settles before the whip.
   - Candidates: The Warning "Without Einstein, your map would walk you into a lake." (the cost, but it spends the payoff's gag in the
     first line); The Statistic "Your phone's map would be ten kilometres off by tonight." (the number before the reason, two ideas).
     The Impossible Claim won: one idea, the title itself, and the "wait, how?" lands at once.
3. **Answer (starts 3.04 s):** "Up in space... time runs FASTER." He floats beside the satellite in a bubble helmet comparing watches;
   its clock's hands whirl (burst, hitstop, punch-in, his double take).
4. **Mechanism, one picture:** "Gravity slows clocks down." (Earth with gravity drawn as rings hugging it; a low clock tagged SLOW, the
   satellite tagged FAST) / "Yours, down here? Dragging." (his own watch on the trail, its hands drooping like wet noodles; he taps it).
5. **The number:** "Up there, satellite clocks gain thirty-eight millionths of a second... every day." A counter: +0.000038 s, then the
   Earth's day and night sweep round three times: DAY 1, 2, 3, +0.000114 s.
6. **Payoff:** "Sounds tiny? That's over ten kilometres of error. A DAY." His phone's map pops up big: the blue dot slides off over the
   lake, 10 KM; he follows it along the jetty and walks off the end: SPLASH; his arm comes up holding the phone high and dry.
7. **The weirdest true fact:** "The first test satellite flew with the fix OFF... in case Einstein was wrong." 1977: a silver drum of a
   satellite, a FIX toggle at OFF with a red lamp, a sticky note IN CASE EINSTEIN'S WRONG; him beside it, still dripping, drops floating
   in his helmet, one eyebrow up.
8. **Button and loop:** "He wasn't." He flips the switch ON (CLUNK, the lamp goes green) and smirks; the picture cuts back to frame 1 and
   the Short loops into "Your phone's GPS needs EINSTEIN." Nothing after the last word (0.69 s to the loop).
9. **Caption question:** "Did you know your phone uses relativity? Yes or no?"
10. **The 8–13 s rule, adapted (story.md §6):** from 5 to 13 s the hiker stays inside the phenomenon (beside the satellite, then his own
    watch on the trail) while the reason lands as ONE picture (clocks at three heights, gravity's rings thick low down); no line explains
    in words what the picture shows.

## Sound design

- **Beds:** the lake's water lapping and a low breeze (under 260 Hz), crickets in the pauses; orbit's low hum and a faint high air.
- **Hits:** the map's ping on frame 1; top blips as the beam climbs and a riser into the poof; a low POOF with fluff on top; the whip into
  orbit; glints as space opens; the clock's whirr (top) and a layered hit + ding after "FASTER"; gravity's throbbing wub with slow low
  tocks against fast high ticks; the dragging watch's heavy tocks, tap-tap on the glass, a brassy "waah" after "Dragging."; the counter's
  pop and three day-sweeps with glints, a ding; a questioning "hm?" after "tiny?"; the map's pop, boots on planks, an error buzz (top) as
  the dot slides, a slide whistle (top) for the fall, a big SPLASH with a layered hit and two bubbles; 1977's radio crackle and computer
  beeps (top), a buzzer after "OFF", the note's paper slap; the CLUNK of the switch and a rising power-up after "He wasn't."
- **Score:** a curious synth groove (Dm Bb F C, 112 bpm) at the lake that returns for the loop; a cold drone in orbit; a heavy low drone
  for gravity; the same groove dragged to 56 bpm under "Yours, down here?"; the groove back up in orbit; a light jetty groove; a slow
  1970s drone for 1977. It drops out for every punchline: FASTER, Dragging, tiny?, A DAY, He wasn't.
- **Mix:** voice first (leveler, gentle compression), effects duck under speech, −14 LUFS, true peak ≤ −1 dBTP after AAC. Every gag
  measured in its gap at −15…−22 dB against the narrator's −15.5; qc_audio: one function word ("in", 9.6 dB) under 10 dB.

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| GPS satellites' clocks run fast against clocks on the ground: slower by ~7 µs/day from their speed (special relativity), faster by ~45 µs/day from the weaker gravity (general relativity), net ~38 µs/day | established | R. Pogge, "Real-World Relativity: The GPS Navigation System", Ohio State University (astronomy.ohio-state.edu/pogge.1/Ast162/Unit5/gps.html); N. Ashby, "Relativity and the Global Positioning System", Physics Today 55(5), 41 (2002), doi 10.1063/1.1485583 |
| "Up in space... time runs faster": at GPS height the gravity effect wins | established (the two effects cancel about 3,000 km up; GPS orbits ~20,000 km up) | Ashby 2002 (figure 4: the net shift for GPS is 4.4645 parts in 10^10) |
| "Gravity slows clocks down" | established (gravitational time dilation; measured on Earth with clocks 33 cm apart, Chou et al., Science 2010) | Ashby 2002; Pogge |
| Uncorrected, over 10 km of error a day | established as the classic estimate: "satellite clock errors building up in just one day would cause navigational errors of more than 11 km" (Ashby); "about 10 kilometers each day" (Pogge). It is the range error of 38 µs of light travel; the Short says "over ten kilometres of error", not that the blue dot itself would move exactly that much | Ashby 2002; Pogge |
| Every GPS clock is set slightly slow before launch (the "factory offset") | established (description and the "Next up" comment) | Ashby 2002 |
| The first test satellite flew with the correction off, in case relativity was wrong | established: at NTS-2's launch (23 June 1977) the size and even the sign of the effect were uncertain and "there were some who doubted that relativistic effects were truths"; a frequency synthesizer was built in, the clock was run about 20 days (Physics Today: three weeks) to measure its rate, it ran fast as predicted to within 1 %, and the correction was then switched on | N. Ashby, "Relativity in the Global Positioning System", Living Reviews in Relativity 6 (2003) §5; Ashby 2002; NRL, "Initial Results of the NAVSTAR GPS NTS-2 Satellite" (DTIC ADA058591, 1978) |

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 10 minutes. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile,
pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with
playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The cinematic lighting runs on the graphics
chip through Chrome's WebGL2; without one the render falls back to the classic look and says so (`look: ...`). The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are
vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). Recorded one-shots come from the repo's shared CC0 folder
`assets/sfx/` (`git sparse-checkout add assets/sfx`; credits in `assets/sfx/CREDITS.md`); this Short uses none.
The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
