**Title:** Why Do You Get DIZZY When You Spin? 💫

**Description:**
You spin in your chair, stop, and the room keeps going. Your head stopped, but the liquid in your inner ears didn't. Subscribe for a new strange question every day.

How it works: deep in each inner ear are three tiny loops of liquid, the semicircular canals, set at right angles so that between them they feel every way your head can turn: nodding, shaking and tilting. When you turn, the liquid lags behind the walls and bends a small jelly flap with hair cells under it, and those cells tell your brain you're turning. Spin for a while and the liquid catches up. Stop, and it keeps going for several seconds, pushing the flap the other way, so your ears still report a spin while your eyes report a still room. That clash is the dizziness, and it's why the room seems to turn. Ballet dancers may learn to turn that signal down: in a 2013 Imperial College London study, 29 ballet dancers felt the spin fade sooner than 20 rowers did, and brain scans showed differences in the areas that handle it. If the room is spinning, sit down until it stops, and don't spin near stairs.

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Each inner ear has three fluid-filled loops (semicircular canals), at right angles, that sense head rotation (nodding, shaking, tilting) | established | Purves et al., *Neuroscience*, 2nd ed., "The Semicircular Canals" (NCBI Bookshelf NBK11078); Popular Science, "Why do I feel dizzy after spinning?" (2018) |
| When the head turns, the fluid (endolymph) lags behind the canal walls and bends a jelly flap (the cupula) over hair cells, which signal the turn to the brain | established | Purves et al. (as above); HowStuffWorks, "What makes you dizzy when you spin?" |
| During a long steady spin the fluid catches up and the signal fades; when you stop, the fluid keeps moving and bends the flap the other way, so the brain is told you are still turning (the other way) | established | Purves et al. (as above); LiveScience, "Why does spinning make you dizzy?" |
| The dizziness is the clash between what the ears report (turning) and what the eyes report (still); the eyes also flick (post-rotatory nystagmus), which makes the room seem to move | established (the clash as a simplification) | Popular Science (2018); Purves et al. (vestibulo-ocular reflex) |
| Ballet dancers' brains may learn to turn the signal down: 29 female ballet dancers and 20 matched rowers were spun in a chair in the dark; the dancers' sense of spinning and their eye reflexes lasted a shorter time, and MRI showed differences in the cerebellum (a smaller area in dancers) and in the cortex | one study (said with "may") | Imperial College London news, 27 Sep 2013, "Ballet dancers' brains adapt to stop them feeling dizzy"; Nigmatullina Y. et al., *Cerebral Cortex*, 2015 (online 2013) |
| Safety (description and caption): sit down until it passes; don't spin near stairs | common sense | — |

Not used: the "drunk spins" (alcohol changes the cupula's density): true, but not for this audience.

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 10 minutes. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile,
pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with
playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The cinematic lighting runs on the graphics
chip through Chrome's WebGL2; without one the render falls back to the classic look and says so (`look: ...`). The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are
vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). Recorded one-shots come from the repo's shared CC0 folder
`assets/sfx/` (`git sparse-checkout add assets/sfx`; credits in `assets/sfx/CREDITS.md`).
The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
