**Title:** Why Can't You HUM With Your Nose Pinched? 🤐

**Description:**
Hum, then pinch your nose shut while you keep humming: the hum dies within a second. A hum is your voice with your lips
shut, so all of its air has to leave through your nose. Pinch your nose and that air piles up in your mouth and puffs your
cheeks, until it pushes back as hard as your lungs push. With no air flowing past your vocal cords, they stop buzzing.
Weirder: humming swirls air in and out of the hidden caves behind your face, your sinuses. In small studies a hum aired
them out far better than plain breathing, and the air coming out of the nose carried about fifteen times more nitric
oxide. Did your hum stop? Yes or no?

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A hum is your voice with the lips shut and the soft palate lowered, so its air leaves through the nose ("air buzzing out your nose... lips shut") | established | phonetics of nasal consonants (/m/); Missouri State phonetician A. Mantie-Kozlowski, quoted in Grunge (2023), "Why it's impossible to hum while holding your nose"; Cleveland Clinic on humming and the nasopharynx |
| Pinch the nose with the lips shut and the air has no way out: it piles up above the vocal folds | established | basic airway anatomy; the aerodynamic voicing constraint (Ohala 1983, "The origin of sound patterns in vocal tract constraints") |
| Voicing (the buzz) needs air flowing through the folds: when the pressure above them rises to match the lungs', the flow and the buzz stop ("pushes back as hard as your lungs. No flow, no buzz") | established | Ohala 1983; Phonetics, LMU Munich (P. Hoole), handouts on the aerodynamics of voicing: voiced closures devoice within tens of milliseconds unless the cavity expands |
| The cheeks give way and puff, which buys the hum a moment more | established | wall compliance and cavity enlargement during voiced closures (Westbury 1983, J. Acoust. Soc. Am.; reviewed in the LMU Munich speech-production chapter) |
| Humming swirls air in and out of the sinuses ("airs out the hidden caves behind your face"); a plain breath barely does | established in small studies | Weitzberg & Lundberg 2002, Am J Respir Crit Care Med 166:144-5 (10 people: nasal nitric oxide up about 15-fold while humming; in a model of the nose and sinuses, one humming breath exchanged nearly all the sinus air, quiet breathing a small fraction); Maniscalco et al. 2003, Eur Respir J (humming's NO rise comes from fast gas exchange with the sinuses); HealthDay, July 2002 |
| Whether daily humming prevents sinusitis | speculative (not said in the Short) | Lundberg in HealthDay 2002: "speculative and must be tested" |
