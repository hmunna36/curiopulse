**Title:** Why Can't Most People WIGGLE Their Ears? 👂

**Description:**
Try it: wiggle your ears without moving your eyebrows. In one survey of 442 people, only about one in five could move an ear on purpose. Everyone still has three tiny muscles on each ear (front, top and back), but our ancestors stopped turning their ears about 25 million years ago, so most brains lost the "remote" for them and fire the eyebrows instead. The wiring isn't gone: lab recordings show those muscles still fire when a sound comes from one side, a tiny twitch on that side, and the top ones work harder when you strain to hear someone at a noisy party. They're still trying. Could you wiggle them? Yes or no?

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| About one in five people can move their ears (22 % could move one ear or the other, 18 % both at once, in 442 people) | established (one survey; other estimates 10–20 %) | Code C, *Perceptual and Motor Skills* 1995, PubMed 7478871; Popular Science 2025 ("10 to 20 percent") |
| Three muscles join each outer ear to the scalp: front, top and back (anterior, superior, posterior auricular) | established | Strauss et al., *eLife* 2020 (EMG from these muscles); Wikipedia "Ear wiggling" |
| Our ancestors lost the use of them about 25 million years ago; the system survives as a "neural fossil" | leading idea (hedged: "about") | Strauss et al., *eLife* 2020;9:e54536 ("for about 25 million years"; "possibly 25 million years ago when lesser apes branched off from Old World monkeys") |
| When you try, your eyebrows move instead | observation; raising the brows and moving the ears go together | Code 1995 ("significant contingency correlations ... between raising eyebrows and moving ears"); a joke in the script, not a mechanism claim |
| A sound to one side makes that ear's muscles fire and the ear twitch, a tiny bit | established (lab study, 2020) | Strauss et al., *eLife* 2020: EMG larger at the ear on the side of the attended sound; the movements are tiny ("one to two orders of magnitude smaller" than wiggling; submillimetre) |
| Straining to hear in noise makes the top muscles work harder | established (one lab study, 20 people) | Schroeer et al., *Front. Neurosci.* 18:1462507 (2025): SAM activity larger in the hardest competing-speaker condition |
| The twitch probably doesn't help you hear | leading idea (said only in the picture's understatement) | Frontiers news, 31 Jan 2025 (Schroeer: "there is probably no perceivable benefit") |

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 10 minutes. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile,
pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with
playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The cinematic lighting runs on the graphics
chip through Chrome's WebGL2; without one the render falls back to the classic look and says so (`look: ...`). The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are
vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). Recorded one-shots come from the repo's shared CC0 folder
`assets/sfx/` (`git sparse-checkout add assets/sfx`; credits in `assets/sfx/CREDITS.md`).
The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
