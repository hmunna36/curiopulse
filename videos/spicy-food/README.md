**Title:** Why Does Spicy Food BURN? 🌶️

**Description:**
You bite one chilli... and your whole mouth is on fire. Except nothing is burning.

Your mouth has heat sensors (a protein called TRPV1) that normally go off at about 43°C and hotter, the kind of heat
that scalds. Capsaicin, the molecule that makes chillies hot, fits into that same sensor like a key and switches it on
with no heat at all. Your brain gets a real "FIRE!" signal, so it does what it does for real heat: you flush and you
sweat. Subscribe for a new strange question every day.

Birds don't feel it: their version of the sensor ignores capsaicin. The leading idea is that the burn keeps
seed-crunching mammals away, while birds swallow the seeds whole and spread them. Bonus: in a 2019 taste test, milk
cooled the burn better than water, and skim milk worked as well as whole.

- **In the pauses** (every loud sound has a pause of the narration to itself): the crunch on frame 1 (the gap before
  "You"), the roar of the flame after "FIRE!", the thermometer's two beeps after "burning.", the alarm bell after
  "alarm.", the spoon's ting, all three alarms after "soup.", the key seating (tk) and the CLICK after "key.", a

  after "meant it.", the bird's tweet, the mouse's bite and squeak, the crosshair's lock, his gulp, the placard's boing.

  under "FIRE!" and "mammals") or above 5 kHz (the sizzle that climbs with the red, the soup's sizzle, the sprinkler

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Nothing is burning": the burn of a chilli is a signal, not heat. Capsaicin does not heat or scorch the mouth; it switches on the nerve endings that report painful heat | established | Caterina, Schumacher, Tominaga, Rosen, Levine & Julius, "The capsaicin receptor: a heat-activated ion channel in the pain pathway", Nature 1997;389:816–824; Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine 2021, press release ("capsaicin, a pungent compound from chili peppers that induces a burning sensation ... a sensor in the nerve endings ... that responds to heat") |
| "Your tongue has sensors for real heat": TRPV1, an ion channel on pain-sensing nerve endings, opens at temperatures felt as painful (threshold about 43 °C; the Nobel committee writes "above 40 °C"). On screen: HEAT SENSOR, ALARM AT 43°C | established | Caterina et al. 1997; Nobel 2021 advanced information; Szolcsányi, "Effect of capsaicin on thermoregulation: an update with new aspects", Temperature 2015;2:277–296 (PMC4843897) |
| "A chilli molecule fits them... like a key": capsaicin binds a pocket in TRPV1 and opens the channel, with no heat needed (on screen: CAPSAICIN, NO HEAT). The key is drawn on the real molecule: a six-sided ring at one end, a long zig-zag tail | established; lock-and-key is the metaphor | Nobel 2021 advanced information ("Capsaicin binds a pocket in TRPV1 located deep in the membrane"); Caterina et al. 1997 |
| "So your brain hears FIRE... and turns on the sprinklers": the nerve sends the same signal as for heat, and the body answers with its heat-loss reactions: flushing and sweating ("gustatory sweating") | established | Szolcsányi 2015 ("In humans, gustatory sweating ... in response to hot chili ingestion has been described since a long time") |
| "Birds can't feel it": the bird version of TRPV1 responds to heat but not to capsaicin; birds eat chillies readily | established | Jordt & Julius, "Molecular basis for species-specific sensitivity to 'hot' chili peppers", Cell 2002;108:421–430 ("Birds are indifferent to the pain-producing effects of capsaicin") |
| "Scientists think the plant aims it... at mammals": "directed deterrence": the burn puts off mammals, whose teeth destroy the seeds, but not birds, which swallow them whole and spread them | leading idea, with field evidence. Said as "Scientists think", and stamped LEADING IDEA on screen. (Capsaicin also protects the seeds from a fungus: Tewksbury et al., PNAS 2008;105:11808) | Tewksbury & Nabhan, "Directed deterrence by capsaicin in chillies", Nature 2001;412:403–404; Jordt & Julius 2002 |
| "And you... ordered extra." | a joke (people are the mammal that seeks the burn out) | — |
| On screen: the thermometer's 37°C; the soup's 80°C | 37 °C is normal body temperature; 80 °C only means "scalding" | — |
| Description only: in a 2019 test, milk cooled the burn better than water, and skim milk worked as well as whole | established for that study; why milk works (fat, or the protein casein) is still open, so the script does not say | Nolden, Lenart & Hayes, "Putting out the fire: efficacy of common beverages in reducing oral burn from capsaicin", Physiology & Behavior 2019;208:112557 (PMC6620146) |

Not used: "water does nothing" (the queue's angle; in the 2019 test water did help, only less than milk), how milk
works, the Scoville scale, tarantula venom on the same sensor, capsaicin pain patches: each is another day's fact.

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 6 minutes; `src/cover.sh` then renders `cover.jpg`. They need
python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile, pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic
chain runs), node with playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The JavaScript libraries
the scenes use are vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). Recorded one-shots come from the repo's shared
CC0 folder `assets/sfx/` (`git sparse-checkout add assets/sfx`; credits in `assets/sfx/CREDITS.md`); this Short uses
none. The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
`src/web/brainy.js` (the brain at its desk) comes from the pins-and-needles Short.
