## The plan (written before any picture; reference/story.md §5)

1. **Length:** short arm, 30–35 s, 66–76 words (`yt.mjs next-slot`: the 2026-10-28 23:30 IST slot); the script has 73
   words and lays out at 32.98 s of voice + 1.0 s tail. **Kind:** TEST (the topic test, kind-2026-10). **Subscribe
   arm:** PILL (the pill test, pill-2026-10: the short silent pill over the last 2.4 s, set by timeline_lib.py).
2. **The test:** pat your head with one hand, rub your tummy in circles with the other, then swap. Most people manage
   the first way with effort and tangle on the swap (both hands patting, or both rubbing).
   - **Hook (The Direct Address, the instruction itself):** "Pat your head. Rub your tummy. Now... SWAP!" ("your" is
     the second word; the tangle happens to the viewer at ~3.3 s, as they swap).
     Candidates: The Question ("Can you pat your head and rub your tummy? Now swap.": slower, the question delays the
     doing); The Impossible Claim ("Your hands can't do two different things. Pat your head..." : a claim the viewer
     can disprove on the first try, so it fails its own "fails when"). The last three test Shorts used The Question
     (one-finger), The Question (tongue-nose), The Direct Address (circle-line): Direct Address is free to use.
   - **Answer (starts 4.17 s):** "Tangled, right? Your brain mails every order... to BOTH hands."
   - **Button:** "Now try it... FASTER." (sends them back to the test; it runs into "Pat your head..." on the loop).
3. **Claims:** the science notes below.
4. **Beats** (Jessica ≈ 2.2 words/s):

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim (as said) | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Doing two different rhythmic movements with the two hands is hard; the hands drift into the same movement ("Tangled, right?") | established (bimanual coupling: in-phase and anti-phase are the stable patterns; a circle with one hand and a line with the other turn into ovals) | Swinnen 2002, *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 3:348–359; the Hebrew University review "Who tells one hand what the other is doing: the neurophysiology of bimanual movements" (ELSC); Diedrichsen et al. 2003 (forces of one hand pulled toward the other's; the coupling much reduced after the callosum is cut) |
| "Your brain mails every order... to BOTH hands": a fraction of each command sent to one hand also goes, as a mirror copy, to the other | leading idea (the neural cross-talk model), with direct evidence in mirror EMG activity | Cattaert, Semjen & Summers 1999, "Simulating a neural cross-talk model for between-hand interference during bimanual circle drawing", *Biological Cybernetics* 81:343–358 ("some fraction of any force command sent to one limb" dispatched "as a mirror image to the other limb"); Beaulé, Tremblay & Théoret 2012, *Neural Plasticity* 2012:627816 (subtle mirror movements in the unused hand of healthy adults when a task is demanding) |
| "So your brain shreds the copy": the brain actively suppresses the mirror copy (the shredder = inhibition between the two halves of the brain) | established in outline (interhemispheric inhibition through the corpus callosum keeps one-handed movements one-handed) | Beaulé, Tremblay & Théoret 2012 ("a mature central nervous system capable of transcallosal IHI is a key factor in controlling unwanted MM") |
| "That's the strain you feel": the harder the task, the more the copy has to be held back | simplification of an established finding (mirror activity grows with effort, difficulty and fatigue; holding two hands apart takes attention) | Beaulé et al. 2012 ("the amount of mirror EMG activity seen in healthy subjects increases if the task is demanding") |
| "Little kids' shredders are still growing: squeeze one hand... the other often squeezes too. Till about age TEN." | established (physiological mirror movements are common in young children and fade with age, sharply between about 5 and 8, normal until about 10; linked to the corpus callosum maturing) | Beaulé et al. 2012 (they "decrease with increasing age until the age of 10 years"); Koerte et al. 2010 and Mayston, Harrison & Stephens 1999 (cited there); the "often" and "about" are the hedges |
| "Now try it... FASTER." (an instruction) | established that faster makes it harder: asymmetric two-handed patterns break down as the movement frequency rises | Kelso 1984 (*Am J Physiol* 246:R1000: anti-phase finger movements switch to in-phase as they speed up); Cattaert et al. 1999 (asymmetric circle drawing "disrupted as movement frequency rises") |

Not used: the split-brain studies (Franz et al. 1996; Kennerley et al. 2002): circle-line already told that story.
A 2006 conference abstract by Wenderoth and colleagues is titled "Patting the head while rubbing the stomach" (UTAS
research portal), but only its title was readable, so nothing here rests on it.

## Sound design

- **Beds:** the office's room tone and the light's hum; inside his head a warm, slow throb; the kitchen's soft room
  tone with two birds at the window (in pauses).
- **Hits:** every pat is a low thud plus a slap's top (both can sit on a word); the rub a cloth swish above 5 kHz;
  a referee's whistle after "SWAP!"; a quick "huh?" after "right?"; the glass sparkles; STAMP (low, on "mails");
  the copy's slide (top); fwoop into the tubes (after "order..."); two glints as the cards land; a tape winding down
  for slow motion (after "back."); the bump on "PAT" (low) and a boing after "too."; the shredder: its motor (under
  280 Hz) and paper (above 5.6 kHz) on every copy it eats, the last one whole after "pat."; a sweat drip after
  "feel."; a steam toot after "shredder..."; the overtime clock ticking faster (top); a power-down after "overtime.";
  the duck's SQUEAK after "hand..."; the jet (top) and SPLAT after "too."; a tiny giggle; the candles (top) and a party
  horn after "TEN."; a fizz into FASTER and the room's whoosh after it.
- **Score:** a bouncy C–F groove for the test; curious A minor–F in the mail room, F–G with a kick for "BOTH hands";
  a dreamy held chord for slow motion; D minor with a kick (sixteenths on "every single pat") for the shredder; a
  tense low cluster for the strain; a frantic A minor–E at 148 then 160 BPM for overtime; a playful G–C, G–D in the
  kitchen; the test groove again and a 164 BPM rush after FASTER into the loop. It drops out for SWAP and the
  tangle, the fwoop, the boing, the last shred, the drip, the toot, the power-down, the splat, the horn and FASTER.
- **Mix:** voice first (leveler, gentle compression), effects duck under speech, −14 LUFS, true peak ≤ −1 dBTP
  after AAC (`levels={"music": -14, "sfx": -13}`, `duck={"music": 0.74}`). Gags measured in their gaps at −20.5 to
  −25.5 dB with the voice at −16.1 (median word); qc_audio: 0 words under 10 dB.

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 5 minutes. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile,
pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with
playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The cinematic lighting runs on the graphics
chip through Chrome's WebGL2; without one the render falls back to the classic look and says so (`look: ...`). The
JavaScript libraries the scenes use are vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). The narration takes
are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt` changes. For a changed
line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set. `src/cover.sh` renders the cover.
World files borrowed from circle-line: `office.js`, `foot.js` (its `fsSt`, `fsInHead`, `fsLegs`); new here:
`pathands.js`, `mailroom.js`, `kitchen.js`.
