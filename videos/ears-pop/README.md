**Title:** Why Do Your Ears POP on a Plane? ✈️

**Description:**
Your plane takes off... and something in your head goes POP. Behind your eardrum is a tiny pocket of air. As the cabin
pressure drops on the climb, that air swells and pushes the eardrum out, until a small tube that runs to the back of
your nose (the eustachian tube) lets the extra out. Subscribe for a new strange question every day.

Landing is harder. Now the pocket is short of air, the eardrum gets sucked inward, and that same suction holds the tube
shut. Swallowing or yawning makes a little muscle pull it open. Babies can't do that on purpose, but pediatricians say
crying can open the tube too.

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Ears "pop" when the pressure on the two sides of the eardrum evens out, through the eustachian tube | established | [ENT Health (American Academy of Otolaryngology): Ears and Altitude (Barotrauma)](https://www.enthealth.org/conditions/ears-and-altitude-barotrauma/); [KidsHealth: Flying and Your Child's Ears](https://kidshealth.org/en/parents/flying-ears.html) |
| Behind the eardrum is an air-filled space (the middle ear) | established | ENT Health (above); [Wikipedia: Eustachian tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eustachian_tube) |
| As the plane climbs, the cabin pressure drops (cabins are held at the equivalent of roughly 6,000–8,000 ft, never above 8,000 ft: about three quarters of sea-level pressure) | established | [Wikipedia: Cabin pressurization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cabin_pressurization) (FAR 25.841) |
| The trapped air expands and pushes the eardrum outward, and on the way up the tube lets the extra air out by itself | established | [Go Flight Medicine (R. Jedick MD): Clearing Your Ears](https://goflightmedicine.com/clearing-ears) ("on ascent, the Eustachian Tubes open spontaneously allowing expanding gases to travel down into the posterior pharynx"); Mirza S, Richardson H. Otic barotrauma from air travel. *J Laryngol Otol* 2005;119:366–370 (the tube vents passively at about 15 mmHg of excess pressure) |
| The tube runs from the middle ear to the back of the nose | established | ENT Health ("connects the back of the nose with the middle ear") |
| Landing is harder: the air doesn't flow back in by itself. The pocket is now at lower pressure than the cabin, the eardrum is pushed inward, and that relative vacuum holds the tube's soft walls together | established | Go Flight Medicine ("During descent, however, the Eustachian Tube will often not open spontaneously... a vacuum like phenomenon as the gas contracts"); [Hearing Health Matters: Barotrauma](https://hearinghealthmatters.org/dizzinessdepot/2015/barotrauma/) ("the vacuum behind the eardrum will keep the eustachian tube walls collapsed"); Mirza & Richardson 2005 (the tube works like a one-way valve); [Wikipedia: Middle ear barotrauma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_ear_barotrauma) |
| Swallowing or yawning opens it: a small muscle (the tensor veli palatini, with the levator veli palatini) pulls the tube open | established | Wikipedia: Eustachian tube; ENT Health ("Swallow. Yawning, chewing gum, or sucking on hard candy can help") |
| Babies can't pop their ears on purpose | established | ENT Health ("Babies cannot intentionally pop their ears, but sucking on a bottle or pacifier can help") |
| Crying can open the tube too | what pediatric sources say; nobody has measured it in a trial, so the script says "can" | [Dr. Alan Greene (pediatrician)](https://www.drgreene.com/qa-articles/flying-infant-hearing-loss) ("A tiny muscle pulls open the eustachian tube only during swallowing, yawning, or crying... this is one instance where crying itself helps to solve the problem"); [Live Science: Why do babies cry on airplanes?](https://www.livescience.com/64714-why-babies-cry-on-airplanes.html) |

Simplifications: "the pocket shrinks" (the amount of air in it stays the same; the rising cabin pressure squeezes it and
bows the eardrum in); the "suction" is the lower pressure inside the pocket; "a little muscle" stands for two; the
"burp" is a joke about air leaving through the tube into the throat, not a sound the ear makes. The script gives the
mechanism, not medical advice: sources recommend a bottle or pacifier for babies on the way down (sucking and
swallowing open the tube more reliably than crying).

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 10 minutes. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile,
pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with
playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are
vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). Recorded one-shots come from the repo's shared CC0 folder
`assets/sfx/` (`git sparse-checkout add assets/sfx`; credits in `assets/sfx/CREDITS.md`); this Short uses none.
The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
The cover is `SC.cover` in `src/web/scenes.js`, rendered from a one-shot copy of the timeline (shots = one `cover` shot).
