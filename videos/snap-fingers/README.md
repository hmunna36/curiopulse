## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| A snap works like a catapult: the thumb presses on the middle finger, skin friction holds it (the latch) while the muscles load it, then it slips | established (high-speed filming + a spring-latch model) | Acharya, Challita, Ilton & Bhamla, "The ultrafast snap of a finger is mediated by skin friction", *J. R. Soc. Interface* 18: 20210672 (2021), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8596009 |
| The pop is the middle finger hitting the palm (weak shock waves, like a clap) | established (the paper's account) | same |
| Squishy fingertip pads grip better: pressing enlarges the contact, which raises friction and lets the snap load | established | same |
| "Barely touching... you never LOADED it": a light press stores almost nothing, so the finger slides off slowly | consistent with the paper's model (the speed follows the force loaded); not a condition it measured, so it is said as the catapult picture, not as a number | same |
| A snap is over in about 7 ms (0.007 s on screen), 20+ times faster than a blink | established (7.1 ± 3.4 ms) | same; Georgia Tech news release, 17 Nov 2021 (chbe.gatech.edu) |
| "The fastest speed-up ever clocked in a human... almost triple a pitcher's arm" | established as measured: peak angular acceleration ~1.6 million °/s², 2.6× a pitch's; a pitcher's arm still reaches a higher SPEED, so the line says speed-up | the paper; Georgia Tech ("almost three times that of a pitcher's arm"); Physics World |
| Metal fingertips can't squish, so the snap flops | established: with metal thimbles the snap was ~3.4× slower (rubber, too sticky, ~2.2× slower; a lubricant ~4× slower) | the paper |
| Some people can't snap | everyday observation; no prevalence data was found, so the Short gives no number | — |
