**Title:** Why Can't You Raise Just ONE Eyebrow? 🤨

**Description:**
Try to raise just one of your eyebrows: for most people both go up. Each half of your brain sends its "lift" signal to the
muscles above BOTH brows, so the two tend to rise as a pair, while your mouth gets a separate line to each side, which is why
half a smile is easy. That double wiring is so reliable that after a stroke on one side of the brain, the forehead usually
still lifts on both sides, and doctors check for exactly that. Some people can lift one brow anyway, and nobody is quite sure
why; holding the other one down with a finger is the classic way to practise. Could you raise just one? Yes or no?

## Science notes

Every claim in the script, how sure science is, and where it comes from. Hedged lines are said as hedges.

| Claim | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| The muscles of the forehead get their commands from both halves of the brain; the lower face mostly from the opposite half only | established (the classical clinical picture; tracing studies in monkeys find it messier, so it is a simplification) | RCEM Learning, "Central connections of the facial nuclei" (rcemlearning.co.uk); UMass Chan StrokeStop, "Corticobulbar tract disease and facial weakness" (umassmed.edu/strokestop); a J Comp Neurol review: "the innervation of the face is bilaterally controlled for the upper part, and mainly contralaterally controlled for the lower part" (boris.unibe.ch/72088); Wikipedia, "Central facial palsy" (the caveats) |
| So a "lift" from either half reaches both brows and they tend to rise as a pair, while a half smile is easy | leading idea (the usual explanation; why some people can lift one brow anyway is not settled) | the sources above; BBC Science Focus, "Why can I raise one eyebrow and not the other?" (Dr Nish Manek, 14 Apr 2023): no consensus (dexterity, a dominant brow, genes), and practice can teach it. Said as a metaphor ("on one puppet string"), and the description says nobody is sure why some can |
| About 30–40 % of people can raise one brow | popular estimate, not traced to a study | BBC Science Focus (above). NOT used in the narration or on screen |
| After a stroke on one side of the brain, the forehead usually still moves on both sides while the lower face droops on the other side; doctors use it to tell a stroke from a facial-nerve palsy | established clinical sign ("forehead sparing"); not always (some strokes mimic a nerve palsy) | RCEM Learning; UMass Chan StrokeStop; Radiopaedia, "Facial palsy"; a Japanese case report of hemispheric strokes with upper-face weakness (neurology-jp.org) — hence "usually" |
| Holding the other brow down with a finger is a way to practise | common advice (anecdotal) | BBC Science Focus (practice); framed in the button as a cheat ("Nobody has to know.") |

## Rebuild

`src/build.sh` rebuilds the MP4 from scratch in about 10 minutes. It needs python3 (numpy, scipy, soundfile,
pyloudnorm, pillow, certifi; pedalboard for the studio mix chain, else the classic chain runs), node with
playwright-core and an installed Chrome, and ffmpeg with libx264/aac. The cinematic lighting runs on the graphics
chip through Chrome's WebGL2; without one the render falls back to the classic look and says so (`look: ...`). The JavaScript libraries the scenes use are
vendored in `src/web/vendor/` (MIT, licences inside). Recorded one-shots come from the repo's shared CC0 folder
`assets/sfx/` (`git sparse-checkout add assets/sfx`; credits in `assets/sfx/CREDITS.md`).
The narration takes are committed in `src/voice/`, so no API call is needed unless a line of `src/script.txt`
changes. For a changed line, run `src/build.sh --synth` with `ELEVENLABS_API_KEY` set.
