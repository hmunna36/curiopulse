ring finger (anyone can try it in two seconds, it fails in a surprising way, viewers argue "I can!" or "I can't" in the
comments, and people already search for it), then the dominant eye (a left-or-right answer, like the nostril's), then
the blind spot (it happens on the screen itself). A refill keeps to tests; anything else goes under Held.

- [ ] Which EYE is your boss? — the test: point at something across the room with both eyes open, then close one eye, then the other: with one of them your finger jumps. He points at a wall clock and his finger leaps away from it; the brain merges both eyes' pictures but leans on one for aiming: the eye that kept your finger still is your dominant eye. Bonus to verify: about two in three people are right-eye dominant, and it doesn't always match the hand you write with. Caption question: "Left or right?"
- [ ] Your eye has a BLIND SPOT: find it now — the test on the screen itself: close your left eye, stare at the X with your right eye, and bring the phone slowly closer: the dot on the right vanishes. He does it with a poster and the dot on the poster disappears; where the optic nerve leaves the eye there are no light sensors at all, and the brain paints the hole over with whatever is around it, so you never notice it. Bonus to verify: an octopus's eye is wired the other way round and has no blind spot. Build note: the X and the dot sit near the two side edges in the upper half (inside the safe area), about 5 cm apart on a phone; check on a real phone that the dot vanishes at about 15-20 cm before building the rest. Caption question: "Did it vanish?"
- [ ] Why do you feel TWO noses? — the test (Aristotle's illusion): cross your middle finger over your index finger and rub the tip of your nose in the gap: you feel two noses. He tries it in a mirror and looks alarmed; the outer sides of those two fingertips never touch one thing at the same time in normal life, so the brain reads the two touches as two noses. Bonus to verify: Aristotle described it about 2,300 years ago, with a pebble. Caption question: "One nose or two?"
- [ ] Can you ROLL your tongue? — the test: try it now. Contrarian: the school book said one gene decides it, and it doesn't: identical twins can differ, and the geneticist who proposed the gene (Alfred Sturtevant, 1940) later said it was wrong. He tries in a mirror and fails while his reflection does it (verify every date and the quotes before scripting; hedge: practice helps some people). Caption question: "Can you? Yes or no."
- [ ] Why can't you HUM with your nose pinched? — the test: hum, then pinch your nose shut while you keep humming: the hum dies within a second. He hums happily in the shower, pinches his nose, and the hum chokes off while his cheeks puff; humming is your voice with the lips shut, so all its air has to go out through the nose; block the nose and the air has nowhere to go, the pressure evens out and the vocal cords stop buzzing. Bonus to verify: humming swirls air through the sinuses (one study: nasal nitric oxide up about fifteenfold while humming, Weitzberg & Lundberg 2002). Caption question: "Did it stop?"
- [ ] Why can't you BREATHE and SWALLOW at the same time? — he tries it mid-gulp on camera and splutters (the viewer's test: a DRY swallow of their own spit while trying to breathe in, never with a drink); air and food share one pipe at the throat, and a flap (the epiglottis) folds over the windpipe on every swallow while breathing pauses. Skip "babies can do both" (disputed) Caption question: "Could you?"
- [ ] The floating FINGER SAUSAGE — the test: hold your two index fingertips together a hand's width in front of your eyes, then look past them at the wall: a little floating sausage appears between them. He does it on a bus and the passenger beside him edges away; each eye sees your fingers in a different spot, and when you focus on the far wall the brain lays the two pictures over each other: the overlap is a stub with a fingertip at each end. Bonus: pull the fingers apart a little and the sausage floats on its own. Caption question: "Did you see it?"
- [ ] Why can't you TICKLE yourself? — the test: try it now, on your palm or your ribs: nothing. He tries, bored; his friend's finger comes in and he collapses laughing; the brain forecasts the touch from its own command and turns the feeling down (Blakemore et al. 1998); a robot that adds a fraction of a second of delay makes your own touch tickle again. The 4 Oct long-form covered this (84 views): reuse its research, not its script. Caption question: "Did it tickle?"
- [ ] Why does your voice sound DEEPER with your fingers in your ears? — the test: plug your ears with your fingers and talk: your voice booms. He tries it mid-phone-call; low sounds travel to your inner ear through your skull bones, and a plugged ear canal traps them instead of letting them leak out (the occlusion effect). Ties to the recorded-voice Short (6 Oct): link it in the description. Caption question: "Deeper or not?"
- [ ] Why does your foot SWITCH direction? — the test, sitting down: circle your right foot clockwise, then draw a 6 in the air with your right hand: your foot flips. He tries it at his desk and his foot betrays him; the brain's movement plans for one side of the body like to run in step, so the hand's circle drags the foot's along (hedged: exactly why is not settled). Caption question: "Could you keep it going?"
- [ ] Why can't you see your own EYES move in a mirror? — the test: in a mirror, look from your left eye to your right eye: you never catch them moving. Then try it in your phone's selfie camera: now you can. He stares into the bathroom mirror, then at his phone; during each eye jump the brain blanks the picture (saccadic masking), and the camera's tiny delay shows you the jump after it is over. Caption question: "Did you catch them?"

- [h] Why do you SHIVER when you're cold? — he steps out of a lake into the wind and his teeth rattle; the brain's thermostat (hypothalamus) orders muscles to twitch fast with no movement to show for it, so nearly all the energy comes out as heat. Fear-trembling is a different thing (adrenaline): one line at most, or leave it out. Goosebumps is already on the channel: do not repeat it. From YouTube Studio's suggestions, 10 Oct 2026
- [h] Why does soda burn your tongue? — he cracks a can and takes a giant gulp, eyes watering; an enzyme on the tongue turns the gas into acid that fires sour and pain sensors; it bites just as hard in a pressure chamber with no bubbles, and climbers on altitude pills lose the tingle. Not "the bubbles popping"
- [h] Why does your STOMACH DROP on a swing? — he crests a giant swing (or a rollercoaster) and his belly floats; in the fall he and his organs drop together, so the usual downward tug inside the belly vanishes and the brain reads the change as a lurch. Hedged: exactly which sensors report it is not settled
- [h] Why does your EYELID twitch? — he is mid-presentation and one eyelid starts tapping Morse code; a tiny patch of the eyelid muscle fires on its own (myokymia); it is linked with tiredness, caffeine and stress, and nobody else can see it as much as you feel it. Hedged throughout: the cause is not established. Say: weeks of twitching, or a twitch that shuts the eye, is for a doctor. From YouTube Studio's suggestions, 10 Oct 2026
- [h] Does shaving make hair grow back THICKER? — he shaves one leg (or half a beard) and watches it like a hawk; no: a razor cuts the hair's fine tip off, so the stub is blunt, feels coarse and looks darker; trials since the 1920s found no change in thickness or speed (verify the trial before quoting a year). "What happens when" shape, settles a thing parents say
- [h] Why does pineapple make your mouth tingle? — he bites a dripping pineapple wedge and his tongue prickles; its enzyme (bromelain) snips the protein that guards your mouth, so the acid stings; fresh pineapple stops jelly setting because it digests the gelatin; hedged: needle-shaped crystals may share the blame. Skip the "erased cannery workers' fingerprints" story (unsourced)
- [h] Why do you see STARS when you stand up too fast? — he leaps off the sofa and the room goes dark at the edges; blood pools in the legs, pressure dips for a few seconds and the brain runs short until vessels tighten and the heart speeds up. Bonus to verify: how a giraffe copes. Say that fainting or frequent head rushes are for a doctor. Keep it apart from the eye-rubbing Short (different cause): make them weeks apart
- [h] Why does coffee wake you up? — he gulps his first coffee, eyes half shut; caffeine jams the brain's "I'm tired" docks (adenosine receptors), so the tiredness signal can't land; plants make caffeine as insect poison, yet lace their nectar with it so bees remember the flower (Science 2013). Not "it gives energy": it only masks sleep pressure
- [h] Why do we have earwax? — he twists a cotton bud in his ear and inspects the haul; the ear canal's skin creeps outward like a conveyor belt, carrying wax, dust and dead skin; one DNA letter decides wet or dry earwax, and dry-wax people rarely get smelly armpits. Say that cotton buds are not for cleaning ears
- [h] Why does asparagus make your pee smell? — he crunches an asparagus spear, then recoils; the body breaks its sulfur acid into smelly gases; about 6 in 10 people surveyed notice no strong smell; hedged: the exact molecules are unconfirmed, and one lab test found only 6 % can't smell it. No exact onset time (never measured)
- [h] Why do pools smell like chlorine? — he pinches his nose and jumps into a pool that reeks; the smell is chlorine that has reacted with pee, sweat and skin (chloramines); by tracking an artificial sweetener, scientists estimated 75 litres of urine in one large public pool (one pool, not the average)
- [h] Why do feet stink? — he yanks off a boot after a long hike and the campsite clears; skin bacteria feast on sweat and dead skin and release a cheesy acid (isovaleric acid); their cousins ripen Limburger cheese. The "mosquitoes love that cheese like feet" fact was used in mosquito-bites (9 Oct 2026): find another bonus fact; no "250,000 sweat glands" figure
